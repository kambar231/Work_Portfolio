Feature 01 Controls · Sensor Fusion · The Raymond Corporation

Pedestrian
Braking

Teaching a forklift to see a person, weigh the risk, and stop itself — before anyone has to. 2024 — Present

The Problem

A warehouse floor is one of the few places where multi-ton machines and people share the same square metre, in motion, all day.

A lift truck operator has limited sightlines and a heavy load. The margin between “noticed in time” and “too late” is fractions of a second. The goal: give the truck its own perception and judgment, so safety does not depend solely on a tired human at the end of a shift.

The Approach

The system fuses machine vision with onboard sensors so the truck can distinguish a person from a pallet, a wall, or a passing shadow. A controller continuously estimates risk from distance and closing speed, then escalates its response in proportion to that risk.

Built close to the metal: embedded C for the real-time loop, MATLAB for modeling and tuning, and CAN to talk to the truck's existing systems — so the intelligence rides on hardware the fleet already trusts.

Feature 02 Simulation · Flight Dynamics · RIT

Flight
Dynamics

Nine aircraft, one rule: never let the math hit a singularity at 30,000 feet.

The Problem

An aircraft's attitude is easy to describe — until it isn't.

Euler angles are intuitive, but they fail catastrophically at certain orientations: pitch toward vertical and two axes collapse into one. That singularity — gimbal lock — is exactly where a flight simulation must not break.

The Approach

Across nine aircraft, attitude was modeled two ways in MATLAB and Simulink: classic Euler-angle models for intuition, and quaternion models for robustness.

The quaternion formulation was chosen as the backbone precisely because it avoids gimbal lock — representing orientation without the degenerate poles that plague Euler angles, so the simulation stays valid through any maneuver.

Feature 03 Dynamics · Chaos · RIT

Double
Pendulum

Modeling deterministic chaos — then proving the model against a rig that refuses to repeat itself.

The Problem

A double pendulum is the textbook face of chaos: fully deterministic, yet wildly sensitive to where it starts.

Two arms, simple physics, and a motion so sensitive that a hair's difference in the starting angle sends it down a completely different path. The challenge is building a model faithful enough to track reality before the two inevitably diverge.

The Approach

The coupled nonlinear equations of motion were derived and integrated numerically, producing a simulation of the pendulum's full trajectory through its state space.

That model was then validated against a physical rig — comparing simulated motion to the real apparatus to confirm the dynamics held up before chaos pulled the two apart.

Feature 04 Simulation · Fluid & Dynamics · RIT

Vacuum
Cannon

How fast can air alone throw a ping-pong ball down a 1.52 m barrel? Solve it before you build it.

The Problem

Atmospheric pressure is free, abundant, and surprisingly violent when you let it do work.

Evacuate a tube, seal a light ball at one end, then break the seal: the atmosphere rushes in and accelerates the ball down the barrel. Predicting the exit velocity means tracking pressure, drag, and acceleration along the entire length of travel.

The Approach

A simulation models the ball's acceleration as it travels the 1.52 metre barrel, integrating the net force from the pressure differential against drag to predict exit velocity.

The result is a design tool: change barrel length or vacuum level, and the model tells you what comes out the muzzle — no live fire required.

Feature 05 FEA · Structural · RIT · Ansys

F-1
Nozzle

Putting the engine that lifted Apollo under the microscope: 7.5 million pounds of thrust, and a demand that nothing fails.

The Problem

The Rocketdyne F-1 remains one of the most powerful liquid-fuel engines ever flown.

Its nozzle endures extraordinary loads — the structure must carry the stresses of 7.5 million pounds of thrust without yielding or failing. The question for analysis is simple to state and demanding to answer: does it hold?

The Approach

A structural finite-element analysis of the F-1 nozzle was performed in Ansys, applying operating loads to the geometry and resolving the resulting stress field across the structure.

The objective: verify no failure under operating stress — confirming the nozzle stays within material limits where it matters most.

Feature 06 Additive · Build · Personal

PrintNC
Router

A CNC machine that is part steel, part 3D-printed, all self-built — a workshop you bootstrap into existence.

The Problem

To make precise parts, you need a precise machine — but precise machines are expensive.

The way out is a classic engineer's move: build the tool that builds the tools. A capable CNC router opens the door to cutting metal and wood at home with real accuracy — if you can make it rigid enough.

The Approach

A PrintNC-style machine built from the ground up: a hybrid steel frame for stiffness and damping, combined with 3D-printed parts for the geometry that would otherwise be hard or costly to fabricate.

Entirely self-built — sourcing, machining, printing, wiring, and tuning a working router that earns its place on the bench.

Feature 07 Additive · Code · Personal

G-code
Slicer

A custom STL → G-code pipeline that asks: what if we sliced at the “wrong” angle on purpose?

The Problem

Every overhang in 3D printing pays a tax: support material that wastes filament and scars the surface.

Standard slicers cut a model into flat, horizontal layers — which is convenient, but not always optimal. Reducing supports might mean rethinking the most basic assumption: the slicing angle itself.

The Approach

A custom slicer written in Python takes an STL mesh and generates G-code directly — a full pipeline owned end to end, with no black box in the middle.

Built as a research vehicle for support reduction: by slicing along non-standard angles instead of flat horizontal layers, it explores how orientation can cut away the need for supports in the first place.

Feature 08 Simulation · Materials · RIT AMPrint Center

Lab
Research

From investment casting and CFD to polymer lithography at the molecular scale — simulation first, always.

The Problem

Some questions are too expensive, too slow, or too small to answer by trial and error alone.

Across the RIT AMPrint Center, the work spanned investment casting, additive manufacturing, and computational fluid dynamics — alongside polymer lithography, where the behavior of interest lives at the molecular level and resists direct observation.

The Approach

The throughline is a simulation-first philosophy: model the system before committing material and machine time. In polymer lithography, that meant molecular-scale simulation with LAMMPS to study behavior ahead of the bench.

Combined with CFD and additive/casting work, the research builds predictive understanding first — then lets the physical experiment confirm it.
